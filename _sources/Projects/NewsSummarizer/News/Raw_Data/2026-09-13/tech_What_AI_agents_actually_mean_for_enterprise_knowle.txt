Enterprise buying has never been simple, but several dynamics are making it genuinely harder. Buying groups today typically involve multiple stakeholders across finance, operations, IT, and procurement, each arriving with their own priorities and often conducting independent research before any vendor conversation begins. Gartner's 2025 research found that 61% of B2B buyers prefer a rep-free buying experience overall, and that buyers largely define their requirements before engaging with vendors at all. By the time a seller is meaningfully involved, the internal conversation has often already shaped how the decision will be made.AdvtThis is where much of the current discussion around AI agents becomes relevant. In enterprise settings, agents are not replacing sellers or subject matter experts. They are increasingly taking on the work of assembling, contextualizing, and delivering knowledge across fragmented buying conversations. In practice, that means less time spent searching for answers and more time ensuring the answers are actually right.One Message Does Not Fit the Whole RoomA larger buying committee means a single value proposition is unlikely to carry the whole deal. A CFO is asking different questions than the operations leader who will live with the implementation, or the procurement team focused on risk and contract terms. Addressing one audience well "while leaving others underserved” is a common reason deals slow down or quietly lose momentum.Part of what makes this difficult is that internal conflict within buying groups is the norm rather than the exception. The same research found that when committees do reach genuine consensus, they are 2.5 times more likely to rate the outcome as a high-quality decision. Alignment is not just a process step.
